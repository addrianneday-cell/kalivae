Creatine isn't just for bodybuilders. Here's what the research actually says

Creatine has earned its reputation in the gym, but that doesn’t mean every longevity claim deserves a spot on the label.

Published 5 November 2026 · 7 min read · Editorial review, not individually medically reviewed
Evidence rating Strong What this means How we grade →
By Kalivae editorial Movement & Longevity

The short version: Creatine can help improve strength and performance alongside resistance training; evidence that it extends human lifespan is not established.

Why it keeps coming up

Creatine helps replenish a short-term energy system that muscles rely on during hard efforts. That is why research has focused on repeated sprints, lifting and other brief, high-intensity activity rather than easy endurance exercise. It is not a stimulant and it does not replace a training program.

Where the evidence is convincing

The National Institutes of Health Office of Dietary Supplements describes numerous clinical trials showing benefits for high-intensity, intermittent exercise. The consistent practical takeaway is modest but meaningful: for many people, creatine monohydrate makes it easier to perform high-quality work during training and, over time, support strength gains. Individual responses vary.

The longevity angle, without the leap

Preserving muscle and function matters as we age, so creatine is understandably part of the healthy-aging conversation. But a possible role in maintaining training quality is not evidence that taking creatine prevents dementia, reverses aging or extends life. Those are different scientific questions.

How to think about buying it

For a healthy adult interested in strength training, basic creatine monohydrate is the research-grounded choice. Many exercise studies use a daily maintenance intake around 3–5 grams. Loading is optional, not mandatory. Paying more for elaborate blends does not automatically buy better results. Check for third-party testing and a clearly stated ingredient list.

Who should be more cautious

Water-weight gain and digestive discomfort can occur. Anyone with kidney disease, relevant medical conditions, or medication concerns should speak with their clinician before supplementing. For everyone else, training, sleep and adequate nutrition still do most of the heavy lifting.

The bottom line

For a healthy adult who lifts, creatine is one of the few supplements whose core claim is well-supported. Whether it also protects the aging brain or extends lifespan is a separate, less settled question. Buy plain creatine monohydrate from a brand that publishes third-party testing, and judge it on your training, not on the label copy.
