Does collagen actually work? What 19 trials say. Draft

Oral collagen is one of the best-selling beauty supplements in the world. The short answer: the human evidence for small improvements in skin hydration and elasticity is real but modest, the trials are nearly all industry-funded, and nothing has been shown for deep wrinkles. Here's the longer version.

Key takeaways

  • Across 19 randomized human trials summarized in 2023, hydrolysed collagen peptides modestly improved skin hydration and elasticity after 8–12 weeks of daily use.1
  • Effect sizes are small. Most participants were women over 40, and many trials were funded by collagen manufacturers.12
  • There's no good evidence collagen softens deep wrinkles. Daily broad-spectrum sunscreen and topical retinoids have far stronger evidence for that.
  • If you already eat 1.0–1.6 g of protein per kilogram of body weight per day, you're unlikely to see much extra benefit from a collagen scoop.
  • Safe for most people. Not for anyone with fish, shellfish or egg allergies (depending on source), or during pregnancy without a clinician's input.

What collagen supplements actually are

Collagen is the main structural protein in skin, bone, cartilage and connective tissue. From your mid-20s your body makes a little less of it each year, and skin eventually reflects that. Oral collagen supplements are almost always hydrolysed collagen peptides: long collagen chains broken down into short peptides that the gut can absorb.

The interesting biology — and the part where the honest uncertainty lives — is what happens after absorption. Some collagen-specific peptides such as proline-hydroxyproline (Pro-Hyp) and hydroxyproline-glycine (Hyp-Gly) do appear in blood after ingestion.3 Whether they act as signals that nudge skin cells to make more of their own collagen, or whether they're simply broken down into amino acids and used like any other dietary protein, is still being worked out.

What the research shows, by outcome

Most of what we know comes from a 2023 systematic review of 19 randomized controlled trials (a total of roughly 1,100 adults, mostly women aged 35–65).1 Trials were 8–24 weeks long and gave participants 2.5–10 g of hydrolysed collagen peptides daily.

The honest caveats

  • Industry funding. Most trials are run or funded by collagen manufacturers. That's not disqualifying, but it earns a conservative reading of the effect size.2
  • Small effects. A 7–10% improvement on a hydration meter is a real statistical result. Whether you'd notice it in the mirror is a different question.
  • Who was studied. The data are strongest for women aged 40–60. We have less to say about younger adults, men, or people with darker skin tones — none of which were well-represented in the pooled trials.
  • Protein baseline matters. If you already hit 1.0–1.6 g of protein per kg of bodyweight each day, you're providing the amino acids your skin needs. A collagen scoop then tops up a system that's already running. If you're routinely under-eating protein, fixing that first will do more than a collagen supplement.

Dose, form and timing

  • Form: hydrolysed collagen peptides (sometimes labelled "collagen hydrolysate" or "HC peptides"). Marine or bovine both work; the trials have used both. Vegan "collagen builders" that contain no actual collagen are a different product with different, much weaker evidence.
  • Dose: 2.5–10 g per day, taken as a single dose. Most trials used around 5 g.1
  • Timing: doesn't appear to matter. Trials have tested morning, with meals, and at bedtime with no clear differences.
  • How long: give it at least 8–12 weeks before judging. Earlier than that and the data suggests you won't see the change.
  • Pair with vitamin C: vitamin C is a cofactor in the body's own collagen synthesis. If your diet is low in fruit and vegetables, 100–200 mg of vitamin C (easily from food) is a reasonable pairing.

What to look for in a product

  • Third-party tested. By a lab not owned by the brand. Powders and collagen peptides have had heavy-metals concerns in the past; a certificate of analysis (COA) is worth looking for.
  • Transparent dose. The label should state the grams of collagen peptides per serving. "Proprietary blend" totals are a flag.
  • Clean ingredient list. Collagen peptides and, at most, a small amount of natural flavouring. Anything else is marketing.
  • Fair price per effective dose. Divide the total grams per bottle by the dose you'll take, then by the price. Many premium brands charge a 2–3× multiple of what plain third-party-tested powders cost.

Who should check with a clinician first

  • Fish, shellfish or egg allergy — marine collagen is from fish, and some formulations are processed in shared facilities.
  • Chronic kidney disease or any protein-restricted diet.
  • Pregnancy or breastfeeding — most trials excluded these groups, so the honest answer is we don't have much data either way.
  • People on blood pressure medication: a few trials have reported small reductions in blood pressure with collagen, which could stack with medication.

The bottom line

Oral collagen peptides earn a Moderate rating on Kalivae. The human trials point to a small, real improvement in skin hydration and elasticity in women aged 40–60 after 8–12 weeks. There's no good evidence of an effect on deep wrinkles, and the strongest skin-ageing interventions by a wide margin remain daily broad-spectrum sunscreen and a topical retinoid.

If those two are already in place and your protein intake is dialled in, a collagen supplement is a reasonable "nice to have". If any of those three fundamentals are missing, that's where your effort pays off first.
