Red light therapy for skin: promising results, important fine print

Red light masks are everywhere. The better question isn’t whether they glow, but whether a particular device has evidence behind it.

Published 5 November 2026 · 7 min read · Editorial review, not individually medically reviewed
Evidence rating Moderate What this means How we grade →
By Kalivae editorial Skin & Recovery

The short version: Some red and near-infrared light devices show cosmetic benefits in trials; outcomes depend on device and protocol.

What the light is supposed to do

Photobiomodulation uses selected wavelengths of light, often red or near-infrared, to influence processes within tissue. A plausible mechanism is useful, but it is not the same as proving that every consumer device delivers meaningful results.

The encouraging part

A 2025 randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled study of a home-use mask using 630-nanometer red and 850-nanometer near-infrared light reported improvements in crow’s-feet assessments over 16 weeks. A 2026 systematic review of eight at-home studies including 365 participants also found encouraging cosmetic signals, while emphasizing inconsistency across treatment methods and the need for larger trials.

Where claims run ahead

Evidence for certain facial appearance outcomes does not demonstrate that a mask boosts human lifespan, “detoxes” cells or treats every form of pain. Devices vary in wavelength, power, treatment duration and eye-safety guidance. A study of one protocol is not a blanket endorsement of another.

What to check before buying

Look for relevant published human studies, a clear use case, technical specifications, safety instructions and realistic treatment schedules. FDA clearance, where relevant, should be checked for the particular device and intended use; it is not proof of every marketing claim.

Make it part of a balanced routine

If you enjoy light therapy, think of it as a possible adjunct to basic skincare, not a replacement for sunscreen, appropriate treatment or medical care. Follow manufacturer eye protection instructions and ask your clinician about photosensitivity or medication-related concerns.

The bottom line

Red light therapy is one of the more interesting new skin tools, but the promising data sits with specific devices used on specific protocols, not with the category as a whole. Treat an at-home panel as an adjunct to sunscreen, retinoids and the fundamentals your dermatologist already recommends. Ask for the clinical data behind any device before the price does the talking.
