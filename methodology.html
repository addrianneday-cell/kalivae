Methodology

How we grade the evidence

Every supplement, ingredient and longevity habit on Kalivae gets one of three ratings — gold, silver or bronze — based on the strength of the human evidence behind it. This page explains exactly what we mean by each tier and how we decide.

Last updated 5 November 2026
Strong

Gold

Multiple well-designed human trials or meta-analyses agree on an effect. The effect size is clinically meaningful. Trials across more than one research group. Most people can expect a result.

Moderate

Silver

Some human data supports an effect, but the trials are small, short, or mixed in direction. Promising, not settled. Worth considering for the right person.

Limited

Bronze

Mostly animal, lab or anecdotal evidence. Interesting mechanism, but not yet shown in people. Reasonable to try only after the stronger-evidence options are in place.

What counts as "human evidence"

We rate the evidence specifically for the outcome we're claiming — skin hydration, not "skin"; strength in adults over 50, not "muscle". That's why one ingredient can earn different grades for different outcomes on the same page.

In order of weight:

  1. Meta-analyses and systematic reviews of randomized controlled trials in people, where the included trials are reasonably consistent.
  2. Individual randomized controlled trials in people, counted at face value when they are well-designed and large enough to detect the claimed effect.
  3. Observational studies in people (cohort, case-control). These can move a bronze to silver but almost never earn gold on their own.
  4. Animal and in-vitro studies. Interesting for mechanism; never enough on their own for more than bronze.

When the field has an obvious conflict of interest — for example, trials funded by a brand selling the ingredient — we say so in the review and lean conservative on the grade.

What makes a good trial, in plain English

  • Placebo controlled. The people in the trial don't know if they're on the real thing. The researchers measuring the outcome don't know either.
  • Randomized. Who gets the real thing is decided by chance, not by the researcher.
  • Pre-registered. The researchers wrote down what they were going to measure before they started, so they can't pick the outcome that happens to look best at the end.
  • Large enough. A trial of 30 people isn't powered to detect a 5% effect. We weight trial size.
  • Long enough. Skin trials under 8 weeks, sleep trials under 4 weeks and longevity trials under 6 months are usually too short to say much.
  • Transparent. The protocol, the data and the funding are all disclosed.

How we pick products

Once an ingredient or practice has a rating, choosing which product to recommend is a separate step. For a product to appear on Kalivae we expect:

  • The form and dose used in the trials that earned the rating — not a smaller dose or a different form that happens to share the name.
  • Independent third-party testing (by a lab that isn't owned by the brand) for identity, potency and contaminants like heavy metals.
  • A fair price per effective dose — not per bottle.
  • A brand that will tell you what's in the capsule, including the fillers.

The Kalivae Recommended badge

The "Kalivae Recommended" faceted-gem badge is our top pick in a category. To earn it, a product needs all three: gold-rated evidence for the claim it's making, independent third-party testing, and a fair price per studied dose. There is at most one Recommended pick per category, and it is set independently of any commercial relationship with the brand. When ratings change

New trials come out. Old ones get retracted. Our rating reflects the state of the evidence on the date at the top of each review. When something material changes we update the review, change the rating if needed, and note the change clearly at the top of the page so you can see what moved and when.

What a rating is not

A gold rating means the evidence for an effect is strong on average, in the people the trials were run in. It does not mean the thing will work for you specifically. Individual results vary. If a rating is going to matter for a real health decision, treat it as one input into a conversation with your clinician, not as medical advice.
